Data and method

Where the numbers come from

Three public government sources, joined by address, read with the limits of each one stated. Every number on this page is loaded from the same build the explorer uses.

Only chemical manufacturing is kept: industry codes starting 325. An EPA plant is kept if any of its filings lists a 325 code.

How plants are joined

EPA and OSHA share no plant ID. Plants are joined on the 5-digit ZIP code plus the street number. When the street number is missing or differs, the join falls back to the ZIP plus the plant's name, and needs at least a third of the name words (leaving out words like "Inc" and "Plant") to agree. Every plant records which rule joined it.

Product types and chemical families

Product type comes from the plant's industry code, which says what the government classes it as making. Older codes (1997-2007) are folded into today's groups. A plant can carry more than one; the first is the one on its own OSHA filing.

Injury rate, and the band chance explains

The injury rate is OSHA's standard one: recordable injuries per 200,000 hours, which is about 100 people working full time for a year.

injury rate = recordable injuries x 200,000 / hours worked

Filings that report fewer than or more than hours per employee are left out of every rate. Those numbers cannot be right, and a single one can swamp a whole peer group (the screen is the one used in ehs-osha-analysis).

Small plants swing a lot by chance. To show how much, the explorer asks: if a plant this size ran at exactly the peer rate, how many injuries would it record? Injury counts are modelled as a Poisson count, whose mean is the peer rate times the plant's hours. The shaded band runs from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of that count, so 19 plants out of 20 that truly run at the peer rate land inside it. The dotted line is the 99.9th percentile. Limits are exact, not a normal approximation, because small plants expect well under five injuries. The drawn edge is smoothed between whole counts.

A plant inside the band cannot be told apart from its peers on injury rate alone. That is the honest reading for most small plants.

Releases

A plant files its accident history with each Risk Management Program submission, covering the five years before it, and must update it within six months of a new accident. A plant-year counts toward release rates only when a filing covers it. Release rates can be divided by plant-years, by workers or by pounds held; the explorer lets you switch, because the choice changes the picture.

Across covered plant-years since 2010, a plant with a release in the previous five years had another the next year as often as a plant without one.

Serious injuries

Severe Injury Reports exist only where federal OSHA covers private employers. Plants in states that run their own OSHA program are left out of every serious-injury comparison: .

Each report is grouped by the kind of energy that caused it, from its event code (BLS OIICS 2.01): caught in machinery, heat and steam, chemical exposure, struck by an object, slips and falls, vehicles and forklifts, fire or explosion, electricity, strain.

What the data cannot tell you

  • It is all self-reported. A plant that under-records looks safer than it is.
  • Correlation, not cause. A plant's release history may predict its next release because of the chemicals it handles, not because of anything it did.
  • Address joins miss plants whose filings use different addresses, and can join two employers that share one address.
  • Releases below EPA's reporting criteria, and near-misses, are not in any public file.
  • Nothing here is an audit of any plant.
