Your injury rate can't warn you about the accident that matters most

We followed 10,597 US chemical plants through federal injury logs, serious-injury reports and EPA chemical accident records, 2016 to 2024.

One injury is the whole score

The injury rate counts recordable injuries per 100 full-time workers a year. It looks precise. At a small plant it mostly measures luck.

A typical plant with 50-99 people works about 138,777 hours a year. One recordable injury moves its rate by 1.44 points. That single injury is 54% of the typical rate for plants that size, 2.68.

At plants with 1,000+ people, the same injury moves the rate by 0.06. In the smallest group (under 50 people) one injury adds 3.53, more than the group's typical rate of 3.01.

Luck looks like skill

At plants with 50-99 people, 33% of plant-years ended with zero recordable injuries. In the smallest group it was 56%. A zero year is common, and at a small plant it says little about the next one.

We checked how well one year's ranking predicts the next year's. If rankings were pure skill, the score would be 1. If they were pure luck, it would be 0. For the smallest plants the score is 0.31. For the largest it is 0.74.

So a small plant near the top of this year's league table has mostly had a good draw. Next year it is likely to slide back toward the middle, even if nothing changed on the floor.

A fairer way to read a small plant

Every plant size has a band that chance alone would produce. Take a plant with 50-99 people, working 138,777 hours at the typical rate for its size. It should expect about 1.9 recordable injuries a year. In 19 years out of 20, chance alone would put its rate anywhere from 0.0 to 6.6.

A plant with 1,000+ people has a narrow band, 0.3 to 1.2. Its rate tells you something every year. The small plant's rate tells you something only when it lands outside its band.

How to read a small plant's rate

  1. Pool three years of hours and injuries before you judge.
  2. Compare the result with the band for your plant's size, not with a league table.
  3. Look hard at plants outside the band, on either side.

Does a low injury rate mean fewer chemical releases?

A 2005 refinery explosion in Texas City killed and injured workers at a site whose injury rate had looked good for years. The investigators' lesson has been repeated since: a low count of cuts, strains and falls says little about whether the chemistry is under control.

We tested that on chemical plants. Within each size group we sorted plant-years into three piles: zero injuries, a lower injury rate than the middle plant, and a higher one. Then we counted how many had a chemical release the next year.

The piles came out about the same. For plants of under 100 people, 1.6% of the lower-rate half had a release the next year, against 1.8% of the higher-rate half. The biggest gap in any size group is 2.4 points, and there it runs the wrong way: at the largest plants the higher-injury half had fewer releases.

The counts at the large plants are small, so we don't read anything into the direction. The plain reading is that last year's injury rate is no useful guide to next year's chemical release.

What does warn you

Across all plant-years in EPA's chemical accident program, the chance of a release the next year is 2.6%. Three things in the records moved that number. None of them is an injury count.

A release in the past five years. Plants with one had another the next year 12.1% of the time. Plants without: 1.5%. That is 8.2 times the chance.

The chemistry itself. By chemical family the chance runs from 1.4% for Isocyanates to 7.3% for Sulfur compounds. Formaldehyde plants sit at 3.8%.

These are patterns, and the causes overlap. A plant with a big inventory is usually also a big, complex plant with many chemicals. We can't separate those strands with this data.

Serious injuries hide behind clean records

18%

of serious injuries (amputations and hospital stays) happened at plants that had logged zero recordable injuries the year before. That is about one in 5, out of 703 serious injuries where we could see the prior year's log.

Does a higher rate at least flag the next serious injury? A little, at mid-size plants. At plants with 100-249 people, the higher-rate half had a serious injury the next year 5.1% of the time, against 3.4% for the lower half. For smaller plants the order flips. For the largest the gap is small.

Plants coming off a zero year did have fewer serious injuries on average. They still account for about one serious injury in 5.

What hurts people at chemical plants

These plants reported 1,913 serious injuries to OSHA between 2015 and November 2025. 628 were amputations.

We sorted each report by the kind of energy that did the harm. Machinery leads by a wide margin: 35% of the injuries. At resin and plastics plants it is 41%.

None of this is about blame. It shows where the energy is, so it can be controlled before someone is near it.

Caught in machinery 35%

Lock it out and try it before you clear a jam, change a screen or reach past a guard. If the machine can move, your hands stay out.

Slips, trips and falls 19%

Keep walkways dry and clear. Use three points of contact on ladders, tank tops and truck beds.

Chemical contact 11%

Confirm a line is drained and isolated before you open it. Wear the face and skin protection the job calls for, every time.

Heat, steam and hot material 10%

Treat a line or vessel as hot until you've checked it. Stand to the side when you crack a flange or open a hatch.

Struck by objects, forklifts and vehicles 10% and 6%

Get eye contact with the driver before you cross. Keep people and forklifts on separate paths where you can.

How releases happen at formaldehyde plants

For resin and formaldehyde teams, here is what EPA's chemical accident program shows. It lists 75 releases that involved formaldehyde itself, with 32 worker injuries reported alongside them.

Spills lead by far. 71% involved a liquid spill and 17% a gas release. The most common sources were process vessels and reactors (35%), storage tanks (25%) and piping (15%).

Plants named human error in 43% of these releases, equipment failure in 31% and a procedure that was wrong or not followed in 25%. "Human error" on a report often stops the question early. The better question is what made the error easy to make.

Afterwards, plants most often retrained people (57%), rewrote procedures (55%) or bought better equipment (44%). Holding less chemical on site came up in 0 of the 75 reports, though inventory is one of the clearest warning signs above.

One release can list several types, sources, causes and changes, so the shares in each panel overlap.

What to do on Monday

For plant leaders

  • Stop ranking small plants on one year's injury rate. Pool three years and compare with the band for the plant's size.
  • Track the measures that do warn you. Release history, near-releases and how much regulated chemical is on site.
  • Review your high-energy tasks. Clearing jams, opening lines, work at height, forklift traffic.
  • Treat a zero year as a result to check, not a reason to relax.

For the floor

  • Lock it out and try it before your hands go near a machine that can move.
  • Check that a line is drained, isolated and cool before you open it.
  • Stop the job when something doesn't look right. You won't be the first to ask.
  • Report the near-miss, the small leak and the valve that sticks. Those reports are the early warning this data says injury counts can't give.

How we did this

Sources

  • OSHA Injury Tracking Application, Form 300A summaries, 2016 to 2024 (osha.gov).
  • OSHA Severe Injury Reports, January 2015 to November 2025 (osha.gov).
  • EPA Risk Management Program accident histories, 1993 to 2025, from the Data Liberation Project's FOIA copy (data-liberation-project.org).

Joining the files

  • Plants are chemical manufacturers (NAICS 325). 10,597 plants in total: 9,178 with OSHA injury logs and 2,773 in the EPA program.
  • Records were joined by normalised street address. 1,354 EPA plants matched an OSHA log (48.8% of EPA plants). 1,210 of 1,913 serious-injury reports matched a plant (63.2%).
  • Injury rates are recordable cases per 200,000 hours. Filings outside 120 to 4,500 hours per employee were dropped as implausible.

Comparisons

  • Serious-injury comparisons leave out the 22 states and territories that run their own OSHA plan, because their reports do not reach the federal file.
  • An EPA accident is on file only if it fell in the five years before one of the plant's submissions, so a plant-year counts for releases only when a submission covers it.
  • "Lower" and "higher" rate halves split the plants with at least one injury at the median rate of their size group. Plants with zero injuries are their own group.
  • Ranking stability is the Spearman rank correlation of a plant's rate in one year with the next. The chance band is a 95% Poisson interval around the size group's pooled rate.
  • Everything here is correlation. The data shows which plants had releases and injuries, not why.
