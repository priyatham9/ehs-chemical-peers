One injury is the whole score

The injury rate counts recordable injuries per 100 full-time workers a year. It looks precise. At a small plant it mostly measures luck.

A typical plant with {{size_noise[1].band}} people works about {{size_noise[1].median_hours}} hours a year. One recordable injury moves its rate by {{size_noise[1].one_case_swing}} points. That single injury is {{derived.swing_vs_rate_small_pct}}% of the typical rate for plants that size, {{size_noise[1].pooled_trir}}.

At plants with {{size_noise[4].band}} people, the same injury moves the rate by {{size_noise[4].one_case_swing}}. In the smallest group ({{size_noise[0].band|lower}} people) one injury adds {{size_noise[0].one_case_swing}}, more than the group's typical rate of {{size_noise[0].pooled_trir}}.

Luck looks like skill

At plants with {{size_noise[1].band}} people, {{size_noise[1].zero_share_pct|0}}% of plant-years ended with zero recordable injuries. In the smallest group it was {{size_noise[0].zero_share_pct|0}}%. A zero year is common, and at a small plant it says little about the next one.

We checked how well one year's ranking predicts the next year's. If rankings were pure skill, the score would be 1. If they were pure luck, it would be 0. For the smallest plants the score is {{stability[0].rho|2}}. For the largest it is {{stability[4].rho|2}}.

So a small plant near the top of this year's league table has mostly had a good draw. Next year it is likely to slide back toward the middle, even if nothing changed on the floor.

A fairer way to read a small plant

Every plant size has a band that chance alone would produce. Take a plant with {{size_noise[1].band}} people, working {{size_noise[1].median_hours}} hours at the typical rate for its size. It should expect about {{derived.band_small_expected}} recordable injuries a year. In 19 years out of 20, chance alone would put its rate anywhere from {{derived.band_small_lo|1}} to {{derived.band_small_hi|1}}.

A plant with {{size_noise[4].band}} people has a narrow band, {{derived.band_large_lo|1}} to {{derived.band_large_hi|1}}. Its rate tells you something every year. The small plant's rate tells you something only when it lands outside its band.

How to read a small plant's rate

  1. Pool three years of hours and injuries before you judge.
  2. Compare the result with the band for your plant's size, not with a league table.
  3. Look hard at plants outside the band, on either side.

Does a low injury rate mean fewer chemical releases?

A 2005 refinery explosion in Texas City killed and injured workers at a site whose injury rate had looked good for years. The investigators' lesson has been repeated since: a low count of cuts, strains and falls says little about whether the chemistry is under control.

We tested that on chemical plants. Within each size group we sorted plant-years into three piles: zero injuries, a lower injury rate than the middle plant, and a higher one. Then we counted how many had a chemical release the next year.

The piles came out about the same. For plants of {{trir_vs_outcomes.release_next_year[0].size|lower}} people, {{trir_vs_outcomes.release_next_year[0].below_median.pct|1}}% of the lower-rate half had a release the next year, against {{trir_vs_outcomes.release_next_year[0].above_median.pct|1}}% of the higher-rate half. The biggest gap in any size group is {{derived.release_gap_max_pts}} points, and there it runs the wrong way: at the largest plants the higher-injury half had fewer releases.

The counts at the large plants are small, so we don't read anything into the direction. The plain reading is that last year's injury rate is no useful guide to next year's chemical release.

What does warn you

Across all plant-years in EPA's chemical accident program, the chance of a release the next year is {{release_predictors.base.pct|1}}%. Three things in the records moved that number. None of them is an injury count.

These are patterns, and the causes overlap. A plant with a big inventory is usually also a big, complex plant with many chemicals. We can't separate those strands with this data.

None of this is about blame. It shows where the energy is, so it can be controlled before someone is near it.

Caught in machinery {{derived.energy_caught_pct|0}}%

What to do on Monday

For plant leaders

  • Stop ranking small plants on one year's injury rate. Pool three years and compare with the band for the plant's size.
  • Track the measures that do warn you. Release history, near-releases and how much regulated chemical is on site.
  • Review your high-energy tasks. Clearing jams, opening lines, work at height, forklift traffic.
  • Treat a zero year as a result to check, not a reason to relax.

For the floor

  • Lock it out and try it before your hands go near a machine that can move.
  • Check that a line is drained, isolated and cool before you open it.
  • Stop the job when something doesn't look right. You won't be the first to ask.
  • Report the near-miss, the small leak and the valve that sticks. Those reports are the early warning this data says injury counts can't give.

How we did this

Sources

  • OSHA Injury Tracking Application, Form 300A summaries, {{derived.ita_first_year}} to {{derived.ita_last_year}} (osha.gov).
  • OSHA Severe Injury Reports, January {{derived.sir_first_year}} to {{derived.sir_last_month}} {{derived.sir_last_year}} (osha.gov).
  • EPA Risk Management Program accident histories, {{derived.rmp_first_year}} to {{derived.rmp_last_year}}, from the Data Liberation Project's FOIA copy (data-liberation-project.org).

Joining the files

  • Plants are chemical manufacturers (NAICS 325). {{coverage.plants}} plants in total: {{coverage.osha_plants}} with OSHA injury logs and {{coverage.rmp_plants}} in the EPA program.
  • Records were joined by normalised street address. {{coverage.linked_plants}} EPA plants matched an OSHA log ({{coverage.rmp_linked_pct|1}}% of EPA plants). {{coverage.serious_injuries_linked}} of {{coverage.serious_injuries}} serious-injury reports matched a plant ({{coverage.serious_injuries_linked_pct|1}}%).
  • Injury rates are recordable cases per 200,000 hours. Filings outside {{derived.min_hours_per_employee}} to {{derived.max_hours_per_employee}} hours per employee were dropped as implausible.

Comparisons

  • Serious-injury comparisons leave out the {{derived.state_plan_count}} states and territories that run their own OSHA plan, because their reports do not reach the federal file.
  • An EPA accident is on file only if it fell in the five years before one of the plant's submissions, so a plant-year counts for releases only when a submission covers it.
  • "Lower" and "higher" rate halves split the plants with at least one injury at the median rate of their size group. Plants with zero injuries are their own group.
  • Ranking stability is the Spearman rank correlation of a plant's rate in one year with the next. The chance band is a 95% Poisson interval around the size group's pooled rate.
  • Everything here is correlation. The data shows which plants had releases and injuries, not why.
